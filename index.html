Chapter 1 of 7Premise

First, test the premise

Before going further, we checked two assumptions built into that question: that half the world’s calories now cross a border, and that those calories are less nourishing.

Claim one holds: the average diet crossed the 50% line in 2020

Imported share = the share of the food calories people eat that comes from imports.

For each of 178 countries we divided the calories that come from imports by the total food calories supplied, then averaged across countries year by year. The share rose every few years without reversal — from 43.0% in 2010 to 50.4% in 2023, a trend of +0.64 points per year (r = 0.98, p < 10⁻⁸). Weighting countries by population gives the same direction, +0.40 points a year, while the population-weighted starch share fell by 0.14 points a year (both p < 0.001). On average, more trade has not meant starchier diets. The problem is where the two overlap.

Claim two holds too: the world ships what keeps

Split the world’s food supply into groups and a pattern appears. The groups traded most heavily — sugar (68% of calories imported), oils (64%), cereals (56%) — carry little iron per calorie. The groups that stay home — vegetables, eggs, roots — carry the most. Sugar, oil and grain travel well and don’t spoil; leafy greens and eggs don’t.

Cereals, oils and sugar make up 60% of the average country’s calories but only 37% of its iron. Vegetables are 2% of calories yet deliver 13% of iron and 26% of vitamin A.

In 82% of countries, home-grown calories are the denser half

No FAO file labels a calorie as imported and reports its nutrients, so the team built a new metric. Each food group’s calories and nutrients are split using that group’s own import share; the pieces are summed; and nutrient density per 1,000 kcal is compared between the imported and domestic halves. Iron, zinc, vitamins A, B12 and C are each scaled to a woman’s daily requirement, then averaged.

146 of 178 countries show a positive gap (median +33% of daily need per 1,000 kcal). Protein, measured separately, tells the same story: imported food carries 26.7 g per 1,000 kcal vs 32.9 g for home-grown, less dense in 131 of 177 countries. Measured by starch, the pattern is stronger still: in 84% of countries, imported calories are more starch-heavy than home-grown ones.

So what

The premise is real: trade has pushed half the world’s plate across borders, and what crosses is the nutritionally thinner half. But trade alone doesn’t make a diet starchy — import share is in fact negatively linked to starch share (r = −0.45; full correlation picture in Chapter 7). Chapter 2 finds where the two overlap.

Chapter 2 of 7Who's trapped

Who has traded their way into the trap

We define the starch trap as a country that is simultaneously in the top third for imported calories, the top third for starch share, and the bottom third for micronutrient supply.

Each condition alone points at the wrong countries. High imports alone catch Macao, the Netherlands and Belgium, which buy a diverse, low-starch diet from abroad. High starch alone catches DR Congo (83% starch, 7% imported) and Madagascar, which grow their own staples, so trade isn’t the mechanism. Only the intersection isolates the story the question asks about: countries where trade plausibly is the route into a starchy, thin diet.

What’s on the boat

The team traced imports item by item for the trap and watch-list countries with the heaviest burdens. More than nine-tenths of what they import is classed as starchy: rice, wheat and maize, with palm oil and sugar riding alongside. These are the calories that keep, ship cheaply, and carry little iron.

So what

The trap is concentrated in small, arid or land-constrained economies in West and Southern Africa, plus Yemen, Iraq, Bhutan and Timor-Leste. Now: do the women living there pay for it?

Chapter 3 of 7Women's outcomes

Does the starch trap show up in women?

Across countries, yes, and it survives the obvious alternative explanations. Anemia in women 15–49 is where a thin diet becomes measurable.

Is it really diet, or is starch standing in for poverty or disease?

Starchy diets go with poverty, and poverty goes with malaria, dirty water and weak health systems. So we added WHO and DHS controls one at a time. Adding malaria leaves 86% of the starch effect in place. Water, sanitation, adolescent fertility and child stunting leave 74%.

The sharpest check is the poverty test. Within the 53 lower-income DHS countries, income stops predicting anemia at all (−1.19, not significant), but starch keeps almost its full-sample effect (+0.298 vs +0.315). Diet composition carries signal exactly where income does not.

Malaria splits the high-anemia countries into two groups

Malaria destroys red blood cells, so it causes anemia whatever people eat. If malaria were quietly driving the trap countries’ anemia, the diet story would fall apart. It isn’t: of the seven trap countries with a malaria figure, six have low rates. The countries combining very high anemia with heavy malaria (Mozambique, Niger, Côte d’Ivoire, Gabon) sit outside the trap. Liberia is the one trap country where malaria is clearly part of the picture, and India has neither a trade nor a malaria explanation.

It’s not how much iron is in the food supply — it’s whether the body can absorb it

Niger’s food supply delivers 24.2 mg of iron per person per day, above the commonly cited 18 mg target, yet 47% of its women are anemic. The reason is absorption, not supply: iron from meat, fish, eggs and dairy (“heme” iron) is efficiently absorbed, while iron from cereals and beans (“non-heme” iron) is not. Only 4% of Niger’s iron comes from animal sources — the rest is the poorly absorbed kind. Across countries, total iron supply barely tracks anemia (r = −0.21). The share of iron that comes from animal sources tracks it 2.7× more strongly (r = −0.55).

The same pattern shows up over time. Between 2010 and 2023 the typical country’s iron supply edged up from 15.4 to 16.0 mg a day, and it rose in 102 of 162 countries. Anemia rose anyway in 73 of those 102. Burkina Faso (24.8 mg a day, 44% anemia) matches Niger’s pattern.

Why this is a women’s story

National food supply is an average, and women often don’t receive the average. Iron needs peak during the reproductive years, nutrient-dense foods cost more, and women are more likely to go without them.

So what

Countries with starchier food supplies have markedly more anemic women, and not simply because they are poorer or have more malaria. Global anemia is also moving the wrong way, so this is not a problem solving itself.

Chapter 4 of 7What didn't hold

What held up, and what didn't

Across three notebooks and fifteen scripts, the team tested roughly nine explanations. Some held up; some collapsed.

Held up

  • Imported calories are thinnerDomestic calories are denser in 146 of 178 countries.
  • Starch share predicts anemia across countries+0.315, still +0.235 with every control; survives the poverty test.
  • Malaria is a major co-driverr = 0.525 with anemia; adding it cuts the income coefficient from −2.85 to −0.35 in the extended notebook.
  • Iron form matters more than iron amountAnimal-source share r = −0.55 vs total iron r = −0.21.

Did not hold

  • Import dependence aloner = −0.18 with anemia. Belgium and Bahrain both import over 96% of calories; their anemia rates are 15% and 38%.
  • Change within a country over timeCountry + year fixed effects, 2,276 country-years: no diet variable significant (all p > 0.13).
  • Diet as a 3-year early warningLagged fixed-effects model: p = 0.22–0.72.
  • Food prices as the channelLocal staple prices are falling in Lesotho (−6%) and Mauritania (−7%). Iron-dense foods got cheaper relative to cereals in 9 of 10 checkable countries.
  • Iron on paper, or iron shipped abroadTotal iron supply doesn’t predict anemia once income is held fixed (p = 0.75), and neither does exporting iron-rich harvests (p = 0.45).

Some of the highest burdens sit outside the trap altogether. India (53.7% anemia) imports only 6.8% of its calories, mainly edible oils, sugar and pulses, even though it is the world’s largest vegetable-oil importer. Gabon has the worst rate in the panel (59.9%) at $18,700 per person, with imports below the threshold. Their drivers are domestic diets, malaria, sanitation and health access, not trade.

Two trap countries, moving in opposite directions

If diet were a quick lever on anemia, a trap country cutting its starch should see anemia fall. Lesotho and Gambia show why the within-country test came up empty.

Economic growth alone doesn’t get a country out of the trap

Income is falling in several countries this story flags, most sharply Afghanistan, where GDP per person fell by nearly a third. But growth is no escape either: Bhutan and Botswana grew fast and are still in the trap.

Trade isn’t the villain; it’s an amplifier. Importing is safe when a country can afford to import diversity. It becomes a trap where imports reinforce an already starchy plate.

So what

The finding is a structural portrait: it shows which countries are at risk today. It does not show that nudging a country’s import mix will move its anemia rate next year. That shapes both the forecast and the recommendations.

Chapter 5 of 72030 outlook

Who’s heading into the trap by 2030

A straight-line extrapolation named two new trap countries. We back-tested it, found it did worse than assuming no change, and replaced the forecast with a watch list.

Step 1 — the naive forecast, and why we didn’t trust it

Fitting each country’s 2010–2023 line and extending it to 2030 puts Haiti and Cabo Verde across all three thresholds. A rolling-origin back-test (fit up to 2016…2021, predict later years) showed the full linear trend is worse than assuming nothing changes. For import share, mean error was 4.45 points vs 3.73; for starch share, 2.37 vs 2.01. The best model damps the trend to 10% of its slope. Under it, no country confidently crosses by 2030, and uncertainty by 2030 is about ±6.7 points on imports and ±4.4 on starch.

Step 2 — the watch list: distance to the trap, measured not guessed

Instead of extrapolating, we measure how far each country is from its last unmet condition, in cross-country standard deviations, and report drift only where it is statistically significant. Morocco, Cabo Verde and Haiti each sit within about a tenth of a standard deviation.

A wider lens finds 47 countries moving toward the trap on two fronts at once, with starch share and import dependence both rising. They include Afghanistan, Samoa, Papua New Guinea, the Philippines and Namibia. Afghanistan shows what that path can look like: anemia in women climbed from 31.6% in 2000 to 45.4% in 2023 as its import dependence grew.

Where diets are still getting starchier

Globally, starch is slowly leaving the plate: its share of calories is falling in 102 of 156 countries. In three trap countries and one watch-list country it is still rising. On current trends Liberia, already the starchiest trap country, reaches 77% of calories from starch by 2030.

Step 3 — where anemia itself is heading

For the outcome, the team tested four forecasting models on held-out 2019–2023 data. A per-country trend anchored at the last observation won (RMSE 1.14 points), beating naive persistence (1.45), fitted-line extrapolation (1.37), a random forest (1.49–2.22) and cross-sectional regression (~8.0). On that model, several watch-list and trap countries are still rising.

So what

Can trade composition serve as an early-warning signal? As a screen, yes: it reliably flags which countries are structurally exposed. As a timer, no: in this data, shifts in a country’s import mix do not precede shifts in its anemia. Use it to decide where to look, then track anemia directly.

Chapter 6 of 7What to do

What to do about the starch trap

The evidence says the risk is structural, concentrated and gendered. So the fixes should target the imported plate where it enters a country, protect what’s grown at home, and reach women directly.

The price case for acting now

Trap countries buy most of their grain abroad, so world prices reach them through the import bill. If the last decade’s pattern continues, wheat rises 17–57% by 2030 depending on grade and method, maize 20–41% and rice 14–19%. Chicken is the exception, falling about 14%, which would make one well-absorbed source of iron cheaper — a reason to move on rebalancing (Action 2) alongside fortification (Action 1) rather than after it.

Local markets are calmer today: staple prices are falling in six of the eight trap and watch-list countries with current data, and prices showed no link to anemia (Chapter 4). History backs that up: between 2010 and 2023, world prices showed no measurable link to what countries ate (r ≈ −0.14, not significant), and only for beef did trade volumes and prices rise together. Read this as budget pressure on import-dependent governments, not as a cause of anemia.

Summary of recommendations

1
Governments of trap countries · port & standards authorities

Fortify the imported staples at the border

  • Make iron, folic acid and vitamin A fortification a condition of import for wheat flour, rice, maize and edible oil — the calories trap countries import most, so the imported half can deliver micronutrients instead of diluting them.
  • Supply is concentrated: the top three exporters ship 68–78% of traded rice, 61–66% of maize and 83–86% of palm oil, so a handful of supplier standards reach most plates.
  • First movers: Gambia and Liberia (rice ≈100% imported), Lesotho (maize and wheat), Djibouti (≈98% of calories).

(Ch. 1, Ch. 2)

2
Trade & finance ministries · public food procurement

Rebalance the import basket toward iron-rich foods

  • Cut tariffs and VAT on pulses, dried fish, eggs and milk powder; use state procurement and food-aid contracts to buy these, not only grain.
  • Spread purchases across several suppliers so a single shock can’t empty the shelf. Chicken is the one iron-rich import projected to get cheaper (about −14% by 2030) — a chance to widen access to well-absorbed iron.
  • Watch concentration risk: 78% of traded lentils come from three exporters, and India buys 79% of its imported beans from Myanmar.

(Ch. 3, Ch. 4)

3
Agriculture ministries · development partners

Invest in home-grown nutrition, not just imports

  • Fund home and school gardens, cold chains and extension services for vegetables, pulses, small livestock and eggs.
  • Screen trade and food-security policy so cheap imports don’t crowd out local crops.
  • For the watch list (Morocco, Cabo Verde, Haiti): this is how to hold the last unmet threshold.

(Ch. 1, Ch. 5)

4
Health & social-protection ministries · NGOs

Put women 15–49 first, directly

  • National supply averages hide who goes without — reach women directly with weekly iron–folic acid supplementation through antenatal care, schools and workplaces.
  • Add cash or vouchers earmarked for eggs, fish, meat and pulses.
  • Measure intake by sex, not just by household.

(Ch. 3)

5
FAO · WHO · WFP · national statistics offices

Run the starch-trap monitor every year

  • Turn this analysis into a standing screen using the FAO Food Balance Sheets FAO already publishes; recompute the three conditions each year.
  • Flag any country within 0.25 SD of its last threshold for a nutrition review (today: Morocco, Cabo Verde, Haiti, El Salvador).
  • Pair the screen with the anchored anemia forecast and the WFP price feed — use it to decide where to act, and confirm when with anemia data itself.

(Ch. 5)

6
Health ministries · researchers

Treat the non-food causes, and close the data gaps

  • Where anemia is high but trade isn’t the route, pair nutrition with malaria control (bed nets, seasonal chemoprevention) and water and sanitation.
  • Split anemia by cause with IHME GBD data and pull a malaria time series.
  • Study when fortification mandates were adopted as a natural experiment.

(Ch. 3, Ch. 4)

Chapter 7 of 7Sources & limits

Sources, method notes & limits

Every number above traces to a file in the team’s findings folder. Here is what to read before citing one.

Limits of this analysis

  1. Supply ≠ intake. FAO data are national per-capita food supply, not what women actually eat, and they miss who gets what within a household.
  2. Associations, not causes. The starch–anemia link is cross-country. Within-country and lagged tests found no effect.
  3. The density gap is compositional. It reflects which food groups are traded, not the quality of imported wheat vs local wheat. Vitamins A and C dominate it.
  4. Missing countries. FAO balance sheets omit several high-anemia countries: Mali (56.5%), Benin (50.0%), Chad (46.5%), Togo (41.5%), Burundi (39.0%). Measured relationships are likely a floor.
  5. Modelled outcome. The World Bank anemia series is partly model-interpolated between surveys, which blunts year-to-year signal. DHS measured anemia covers 49 countries.
  6. Hand-built crosswalk. 99 FBS item codes were mapped to 15 SUA food groups by the team, not by FAO. Every decision is documented in the pipeline doc.
  7. Many tests. Around nine variables were tried; malaria and starch are large enough effects to be unlikely flukes, but they deserve held-out validation.
  8. Malaria is one year (2021) and missing for five trap countries, so it explains differences between countries, not change over time.
  9. Price projections are simple extrapolations of world US-dollar prices, not adjusted for inflation and not what any country pays.

Supporting detail: the full correlation picture

Chapter 1 notes that the obvious chain — more imports, starchier diet, more anemia — doesn’t run in a straight line. Here is the full matrix behind that claim. Import share is negatively linked to starch share (r = −0.45) and barely linked to anemia at all (r = −0.18). Starch share is the variable that tracks anemia (+0.64), though it is tangled with income (−0.76). The rest of the story is spent separating those threads.
